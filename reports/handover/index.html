Start here

The one-paragraph version

Home routers that run CAKE, the best open-source queue manager, already keep most calls and games smooth by sharing the line fairly between devices. They fail in one common situation: a call or a game on a device that is also downloading, backing up or sharing files. Fairness then gives the call a small slice of that device's share, and nothing tells CAKE which connection is the call. Existing tools guess with thresholds ("many packets per second means bulk") and get calls and cloud games wrong, because those send many packets too. We built a small classifier that recognises the kind of traffic from the sizes and timing of a connection's first eight packets, without reading any content, and a decision rule that puts each connection in the CAKE priority queue that does the least expected harm. In emulated homes it fixes that failure case, ties CAKE everywhere else, and has never done worse than the threshold tools.

The interim results page shows all of this with charts: Adaptive QoS Engine, interim case study. Treat it as the source of facts and wording, not as the design to copy.

The claim

What we may say, word for word

A safe, low-cost upgrade for routers that already run CAKE: it fixes the one case plain CAKE cannot, a call or a game sharing its device with bulk traffic, and ties CAKE everywhere else. Demonstrated in emulation against plain CAKE and against threshold rules; validation on real router hardware is the next step.

Say

  • "Beats plain CAKE when a call or game shares its device with bulk traffic; ties it otherwise."
  • "Never did worse than threshold rules in any run."
  • "Classifies from packet sizes and timing; never reads content."
  • "Falls back to plain CAKE within about two seconds if it fails."
  • "In emulation" or "in a testbed", every time a live result is quoted.
  • Credit the line-speed scenario (S2) to the capacity estimator, not the classifier.

Do not say

  • "Beats CAKE" without the condition, or "CAKE is bad". CAKE is our floor and we build on it.
  • "A viable upgrade for homes" or "deployed". It has not run on a real router or in a real home.
  • "Seven times better" as a headline. It depends on our scenario mix.
  • "AI", "deep learning" or "neural network". The model is gradient-boosted decision trees.
  • "94% accurate" alone. Accuracy is dominated by web traffic; give per-class figures or say "on held-out flows".
  • That the WhatsApp calls were classified correctly. The video calls were labelled as voice calls, though every one was still protected.

Suggested story

A sequence that matches the evidence

  1. The everyday failure. A cloud game on a PC that starts a game update. Plain CAKE: 83% of the game's frames late. Use the S7 timeline chart.
  2. Why fairness cannot fix it, and why thresholds make it worse. The game is one of nine connections on its PC; threshold rules read its packet rate as a download.
  3. The idea. Recognise the traffic from its first eight packets; choose the queue by expected harm, not by the most likely label.
  4. How it stays safe. Safety shield, fallback to plain CAKE when unsure, watchdog within two seconds.
  5. The test. Nine emulated home situations, five router setups, identical traffic per seed.
  6. Results in what people experience. Late frames and latency first, the summary cost scale second.
  7. Where it does not help, and why that was predicted.
  8. What we learned. Each scenario that exposed a wrong assumption, and the fix.
  9. Limits (the list below, all of it), then next steps: real hardware first.

Must appear in the case study

Limits that have to be stated

Judges will look for these; stating them first is what makes the rest credible. Each is explained on the interim page.

  1. Everything ran in emulation on one laptop: no real router, no real home, no Wi-Fi.
  2. Generated calls and games do not lower their quality when squeezed, as real ones do, so the size of CAKE's failures is overstated.
  3. The model does much worse on data sources it never saw. On unseen video-call apps, 16% of records went to the lowest-priority queue.
  4. The system was tuned partly on the scenarios it is measured on. The final numbers come with fresh seeds and new scenarios run once, to answer this.
  5. The summary cost scale is the one the decision rule optimises; the raw measurements are the evidence.
  6. Expected damage has beaten the simpler top-label rule in only one live scenario so far.
  7. Not yet measured: CPU on a router, a flood of short connections, fairness between devices, IPv6 end to end, VPN traffic.
  8. The adaptive line-speed comparison (S2) still needs the community tool, cake-autorate, as a baseline.

Words

Glossary for writing

CAKE
The queue manager in Linux that most enthusiast home routers (OpenWrt) use. It keeps delay low and shares the line fairly. Our system only gives it hints.
Tin
One of CAKE's four priority queues: Voice, Video, Best Effort, Bulk. In prose, "priority queue" is clearer.
Flow, connection
One conversation between two programs, such as the video of a call or one part of a download. Use "connection" for general readers.
Late frames
Video or game frames that arrived too late to show (100 ms for calls, 50 ms for games). The most readable measure of what a person sees.
Expected damage
For each queue, the harm of putting the connection there, averaged over what it might be, weighted by how likely each is. The system picks the queue with the least.
Damage table
The measured harm of putting each kind of traffic in each queue. 240 testbed runs.
Top label
The simpler rule: put the connection in the queue of its most likely kind.
Seed
A number that fixes every random detail of a test, so all router setups face identical traffic.
Plain CAKE, B2
The baseline to beat. Configuration names B0, B2, B4, O1, O2 are internal; prefer the plain names on the left of the colour list.
Emulation, testbed
Virtual home networks on one laptop, running the real Linux networking code at real speed. Not a simulation, but not a real home either.
