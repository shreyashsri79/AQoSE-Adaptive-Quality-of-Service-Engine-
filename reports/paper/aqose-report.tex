\section*{Summary}

Home routers that run CAKE, the strongest open-source queue manager, already keep most calls and games smooth: CAKE shares the line fairly between devices and then between each device's connections. It fails in one common situation: \emph{a call or a game on a device that is also downloading, uploading or sharing files}. Fairness then gives the call a small slice of its own device's share, and nothing tells CAKE which connection is the call. Existing tools sort traffic with fixed thresholds (``many packets per second means bulk''), which mistake video calls and cloud games for downloads because they also send many packets.

AQoSE is an add-on to CAKE built on three ideas (Figure~\ref{fig:ideas}):
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=3pt]
\item \textbf{Classify once, cheaply.} From the sizes and timing of a new connection's first eight packets (headers only, never content), a small tree model gives the odds of seven kinds of traffic in about 6\,\textmu s.
\item \textbf{Decide by expected harm, not by the top guess.} The odds are weighed against the measured harm of putting each kind of traffic in each of CAKE's four priority tins, and the whole connection goes to the tin with the lowest expected harm. A connection that is 55\% backup and 45\% game goes to Video, not Bulk: if it is the game, Bulk would ruin it; if it is the backup, Video costs little.
\item \textbf{Re-check cheaply.} Each second a model-free check compares the connection with its class's normal range; the classifier runs again on only 3\% of those checks.
\end{enumerate}
The choice is written once as a connection label that CAKE reads for every packet. A safety shield checks every change, a verifier undoes changes that raise delay, and a separate watchdog returns the router to plain CAKE within about two seconds if AQoSE stops.

\section{The problem}

\paragraph{What CAKE already does well} CAKE (Common Applications Kept Enhanced) shapes traffic just below the line rate so that queues build in the router, where they can be managed, and not in the modem's oversized buffer. It shares the link fairly between hosts, then between each host's flows. In our testbed this alone takes a call's late frames from 77\% (no queue management) to 0\% whenever the call is alone on its device (scenarios S1, S4, S10).

\paragraph{Where it fails} Fairness protects a call from \emph{other} devices, not from its own. A laptop on an HD video call that is also running a file-sharing client with twenty connections gives the call one twenty-first of its share. In scenario S6 that leaves 94\% of the call's frames late. The same happens to a cloud game on a PC fetching a game update (S7, 83\% late).

\paragraph{Why tins are not used today} CAKE's \texttt{diffserv4} mode has four priority tins: Voice, Video, Best Effort and Bulk. Something must decide which flow belongs in which. Applications rarely set priority marks that survive the internet, and the tools that sort traffic on home routers (qosify on OpenWrt, for example) use thresholds on packet rate and size. An HD video call sends 400--600 packets a second and a cloud game over 1,000, so those rules call both ``bulk''. On our sealed test set, qosify's default rules sent 78\% of cloud-gaming flows and 14\% of video-call flows to Bulk.

\section{Design}

\subsection{The three ideas}
Three ideas carry the design. Everything else in this section supports them.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=5pt]
\item \textbf{Classify once, and cheaply.} A connection is recognised from the headers of its first eight packets alone: their sizes, the gaps between them, ports and protocol. That is about 6\,\textmu s of work, once per connection, and it happens within the first fraction of a second, before a call or a game has had time to suffer.
\item \textbf{Decide by expected harm, and send the whole connection to one tin.} The classifier gives odds, not a single answer. For each of CAKE's four tins the decision engine multiplies those odds by the measured harm of putting each kind of traffic there, and sends the connection, all of its packets, to the tin with the lowest total. The odds decide which tin; they never split a connection between tins, which would reorder its packets and hurt calls and downloads alike. When the model is unsure the total hedges: a connection that is 45\% game and 55\% backup goes to Video (expected harm 1.10), not Bulk (2.70), because if it is the game, Bulk would ruin it, and if it is the backup, Video costs it little.
\item \textbf{Re-check cheaply.} After the first decision, a check each second compares the connection's traffic, read from the kernel's counters, with the normal range of its class. No model runs. The classifier is asked again only when a connection stays outside its range for 2 to 6 seconds, or at a routine recheck every 30 to 60 seconds. Over 80 runs, 165,684 per-second checks led to 5,453 classifications: 3.3\%.
\end{enumerate}

Figure~\ref{fig:ideas}, in the Summary, follows two connections through all three ideas with the real numbers from the measured damage table.

\subsection{Principle: no AQoSE code on the per-packet path}

The kernel forwards every packet and CAKE queues it. AQoSE writes one label per connection into the kernel's connection tracker (conntrack), and CAKE reads that label for each packet. If AQoSE is slow, wrong or dead, packets still flow; an unlabelled connection is exactly plain CAKE. Figure~\ref{fig:flow} follows one connection from start to end.

\paragraph{Marking} The label is the low three bits of the conntrack mark; CAKE's \texttt{fwmark 0x7} option maps mark $n$ to tin $n$ (1 Bulk, 2 Best Effort, 3 Video, 4 Voice). On upload a postrouting rule copies the label to each packet; on download, packets are redirected to an IFB device whose filter restores it (\texttt{ctinfo cpmark 0x7}) before a second CAKE instance. Mark 0 falls through to CAKE's own DSCP handling, so ``unlabelled equals plain CAKE'' holds by construction, and removing two rules turns the whole system off. Verified in the testbed for both directions, IPv4 and IPv6.

\paragraph{Feature extractor} One nftables rule, \texttt{ct packets < 9 log group 5 snaplen 64}, sends the first eight packets of every flow to the extractor, headers only; the kernel does the filtering. Packets are timestamped by the kernel. Flow and host identifiers are keyed hashes; addresses never leave the controller. Once a second the controller reads conntrack counters for the nine window features. The same Go package runs offline on captures, so training and live features come from one code path (120 of 120 flows identical).

\paragraph{Classifier} LightGBM gradient-boosted trees: 37 boosting rounds for each of 7 classes, 259 trees in all, up to 127 leaves each, learning rate 0.05. Probabilities are calibrated by temperature scaling ($p = \mathrm{softmax}(1.125\,z)$). The trees are exported as data and evaluated in Go; on 2,000 records Go and Python agree to $4\times10^{-16}$ and give the same tin every time. One classification takes about 6\,\textmu s in Go, against about 2\,ms in the Python service.

\paragraph{Decision engine} For each tin $t$, expected damage is
\[ E[t] \;=\; \sum_{c} P(c)\, D[c][t], \]
where $D$ is a measured damage table (Table~\ref{tab:damage}). The lowest allowed $E[t]$ wins. Rules on top:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Voice bar}: Voice only if $P(\text{voice call}) + P(\text{game}) > 0.5$. The table rates Voice as free for most classes, because each was measured alone there; Voice is a quarter of the link.
\item \emph{Demotion bar}: Bulk only if the classes whose best tin is Bulk have summed probability over 0.8.
\item \emph{Bulk guard}: never demote on a first-packets record, and never a UDP flow. On held-out data sources it cut unseen calls, games and downloads sent to Bulk from 16\%, 2\% and 10\% to 0\%, at the cost of leaving UDP peer-to-peer in Best Effort.
\item \emph{Ties}: tins within 0.05 are tied; a tie goes to the tin RFC 4594 gives the likeliest class, never to Bulk.
\item \emph{Leases}: on a device the user prioritises, the damage of interactive classes in Best Effort or Bulk is multiplied by 5.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Worked example} A flow rated 45\% game and 55\% background transfer: Voice $0.45\cdot0+0.55\cdot2=1.10$ (barred, since $0.45 \le 0.5$); Video $1.10$; Best Effort $0.45\cdot2.5+0.55\cdot1.5=1.95$; Bulk $0.45\cdot6=2.70$. Expected damage chooses Video. Top label would choose Bulk, and the demotion bar would hold it in Best Effort.

\paragraph{Safety shield} A pure function: proposal and current state in, accept, clamp or reject out. It rejects anything invalid against the JSON contract, stale proposals, a third tin change within 60\,s for one flow (the flow is then pinned unlabelled), and more than 50 re-marks per second; it clamps shaper rates to their bounds and to steps of $+10\%$ / $-50\%$, and allows an increase only 10\,s after the last. Go's fuzzer generated 10.9 million proposals in 5 minutes with no violation; six bugs planted on purpose were each found in under a second.

\paragraph{Enforcer} Every change is a transaction: snapshot, apply, read back, compare; a mismatch restores the snapshot. Every proposal is logged with its verdict and a plain-English reason, for example ``Looks like a video call (62\% confident); the Video tin has the lowest expected damage.''

\paragraph{Reclassifier} Each class has an envelope: the 5th to 95th percentile of each window feature over its training flows. A flow whose window lies outside on two or more features for two seconds (Voice, Video) or about six seconds (Best Effort, Bulk) is classified again; triggers back off from 5 to 40\,s, and every flow is rechecked at a random moment 30--60\,s after its last answer.

\paragraph{QoE verifier} Only what a router can see: CAKE's Voice and Video tin delay and the probe delay. Ten seconds before a promotion are compared with two 5\,s windows after; if delay rose by more than 5\,ms and more than half in both, the change is undone through the shield.

\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.66\linewidth]{diagrams/loop.pdf}
\caption{The classify and reclassify loop. A flow is classified once from its first eight packets, then watched every second against its class's normal range. A flow that leaves the range for long enough, or whose routine recheck is due, is classified again from a window record; a promotion into Voice or Video is checked by the verifier ten seconds later.}
\label{fig:loop}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Capacity estimator} Three UDP reflectors probed every 100\,ms; delay above a slowly rising baseline signals a queue beyond the router. The download is cut to 90\% of what got through; the upload, whose delivered rate CAKE cannot see, by a fixed quarter. Because round-trip probes cannot tell which direction is queueing, it cuts the download first and touches the upload only if delay persists 2\,s later. Raises are one 10\% step every 10.5\,s at most; with the reflectors silent it changes nothing.

\paragraph{Watchdog} The controller touches a heartbeat file every 250\,ms. If the file is older than 2\,s, the watchdog removes the marking rules and replaces both CAKE instances with plain CAKE at fixed safe rates.

\paragraph{Intent leases and overrides} A small HTTP API in the controller, with a one-page form, lets a person prioritise a device for up to two hours (at most two leases at once). A lease is the user's override of the classifier for that device: it multiplies the damage of leaving its calls and games in Best Effort or Bulk by five, so an uncertain flow on that device is promoted. An operator can also set a flow's tin directly with \texttt{aqos-ctl}. A lease takes effect at the flow's next window record, up to 5\,s later.

\subsection{The safety net}
Every change, whoever proposes it, passes the same chain of checks (Figure~\ref{fig:safety}). Checks that fail reject the change, and nothing happens; bounds on the shaper rate clamp it instead. \subsection{How the line is shared between the four tins}
AQoSE never sets a bandwidth itself. It only chooses a tin; CAKE's \texttt{diffserv4} mode then shares the shaped rate as in Figure~\ref{fig:tins} and Table~\ref{tab:tins}. \section{Data and model}

\subsection{Dataset}
Version 2 has 246,274 records (first-packets records and five-second windows) from seven sources: ISCX VPN-nonVPN and Tor/non-Tor (2016), the Purdue video-call set (Zoom, Meet, Teams and Webex, 2026), UNSW cloud gaming (2025), Labayen (2020), traffic from the testbed's own generators, and our own captures (JioGamesCloud sessions and six WhatsApp calls on a home line). Records are split by session, never by record: 156,049 for training, 29,250 for calibration, 43,221 for validation, and a sealed test set of 1,213 flows from 183 sessions, opened once, after the system was frozen. Each flow counts once in training however many records it has, and each class counts the same.

Public datasets carry their lab's fingerprint: one sends all streaming through a proxy and all bulk transfers over SSH, so a model trained on it alone would learn the lab. Hence several sources per class, a check of each source before use, and own captures.

\subsection{Model choice: LightGBM over a random forest}
Both were trained on the same splits and judged by the rule fixed before training: class-balanced expected damage on validation, through the real decision engine. On the v1 validation set they tied (0.141 against 0.146; accuracy 0.944 against 0.942). LightGBM answers in a tenth of the time (1.4\,ms against 13.5\,ms per record in Python) and is an eighth of the size (1.5\,MB against 11.7\,MB). On a router those two decide it.

\section{How it was tested}

\subsection{Testbed}
One user namespace holds a network namespace per home device, a router, an ISP bottleneck (a token bucket with a 500\,ms FIFO, the ``bloated modem''), a delay element and the far-end servers. No root privileges are needed. Offloads are off on every link so the router sees real packet sizes. The bottleneck's rate error measured 0.00\%.

\subsection{Traffic and measurement}
Eleven generator profiles imitate calls, games, cloud games, adaptive video, browsing, downloads, game updates, backups and peer-to-peer. Every packet carries its sequence number, send time and frame number; a frame is late if its last packet arrives more than 100\,ms (calls) or 50\,ms (games) after it was sent, or never. A plan is a pure function of scenario, entry and seed, so every configuration sees byte-identical traffic for a given seed. A video call can also replay a recorded trace: the real sizes, timing and first packets of a captured call.

On October 15 at 07:41 the system was frozen (code, model, damage table and every setting). All results in Section~\ref{sec:results} come from the frozen system: seeds 0--9 rerun; fresh seeds 10--14 and S11 run once; S12, S13 and S14 run once. All 492 runs after the freeze were valid.

\section{From prototype to product}
\paragraph{Security and privacy} AQoSE reads packet headers only, never payload, and never decrypts. Flow and device identifiers are keyed hashes; addresses never leave the controller, and the action log holds only hashed ids. Nothing in the system needs a credential, and the repository holds none (its full history was scanned before it was made public; API tokens and cookies are excluded by \texttt{.gitignore}). The lease API has no authentication in the prototype; it listens on the router's loopback address in the testbed. A product needs the router's own administrator login over HTTPS, rate limits on the API, and a signed model file: today the model's version and the hashes of its dataset and damage table are recorded, but the file is not signed. In the testbed everything runs without root in a user namespace; on a router the controller needs network-administration capability for tc, nftables and conntrack, and nothing more.

\paragraph{Performance} On the laptop the controller uses 3.6\% of one core and the classifier 0.04\%. A router's cores are several times slower, so we expect several times these figures; measuring them is the first next step. At high line rates on small routers, CAKE's own cost is likely to be the first limit, not AQoSE's.

\paragraph{Hardware} OpenWrt routers already ship CAKE, nftables, the \texttt{ctinfo} action and IFB devices; the Go programs cross-compile to their processors as single files, and the model is 2\,MB of data. Hardware or software flow offloading must be off, since offloaded packets bypass the queue that CAKE manages. ISP-supplied routers with closed firmware would need the vendor's integration.

\paragraph{Operations} Models are versioned directories, and every run records the model, damage table and code version it used, so a model update can be rolled out to a few routers first, compared on tin delay and rollback counts, and rolled back. The watchdog is the kill switch. Telemetry, if any, should be opt-in and aggregate (counts of decisions and tin delays, no addresses). Thresholds, bounds and the damage table are configuration files, not code.

\paragraph{Not assessed} Certification of a router product, regulatory review of traffic management in an ISP-deployed version, and integration with proprietary router firmware or hardware offload engines.

\section{What we learned}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A tin only matters when the flow has company on its device.} The first damage measurements had each flow alone, and every tin looked alike. That reshaped the scenarios and the claim.
\item \textbf{A wrong label is not a wrong tin.} Calling a video call a voice call harms nothing; both are protected. We measure placements, not only labels.
\item \textbf{The damage table was wrong three times}, each time exposed by a scenario: no reward for demoting a backup beside a call; a call apparently better off in Bulk; Voice rated free for everything.
\item \textbf{Simulations share their author's blind spots.} The capacity estimator passed every simulated test and failed four times on the real testbed; each failure is now a regression test.
\item \textbf{With nothing to go on, do nothing.} Lowering the rate when the probes went silent broke a call on a healthy line; the estimator now holds still.
\item \textbf{Public data is not automatically good data}: each source carries its lab's fingerprint.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
\newpagenext
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Emulation only.} One laptop, emulated links from 4 to 100\,Mbit/s; no real router, no real home, no Wi-Fi.
\item \textbf{Generated and replayed calls do not adapt.} A real app lowers its bitrate when squeezed; ours lose frames. CAKE's failures are therefore larger here than they would be for real apps.
\item \textbf{Weak across unseen sources.} Leave-one-source-out damage is high; the bulk guard keeps such traffic out of Bulk, but a call can still be left in Best Effort.
\item \textbf{Partly tuned on its own tests.} Seeds 0--9 include those the fixes were found on; the fresh seeds, S11 and the real-call scenarios are the answer, and they are few.
\item \textbf{Thin classes.} 6 voice-call, 8 streaming and 4 large-download flows in the sealed test set.
\item \textbf{The cost scale is self-graded}: the same scale the damage table is built from. The raw measurements (late frames, latency) are the evidence.
\item \textbf{Voice tin share.} A cloud game above a quarter of the line loses priority in Voice (one fresh seed of S7).
\item \textbf{VPN traffic is out of scope.} A VPN carries all of a device's connections inside one encrypted tunnel, so no classifier on the router, AQoSE included, can tell a call from a backup inside it. We did not test VPN traffic. Tunnels over UDP, which most modern VPNs use, are never demoted and so get plain CAKE; tunnels over TCP must be excluded from demotion before any deployment.
\item \textbf{Probe targets returning.} In S10, when the delay reflectors came back after an outage, the estimator read their return as a queue and halved the download rate for about 30\,s; the download lost 10\% of its throughput, the call nothing. A fix is needed.
\item \textbf{Not measured:} a flood of short flows, gigabit rates, router CPU and memory.
\item \textbf{Leases} take effect at the flow's next window, up to 5\,s, not the 1\,s target.
\end{itemize}

\section{Next steps: from emulation to real homes}
Everything so far ran in emulation. These are the steps that would turn the prototype into something installed and tested in homes, each with how it would be tested and what would count as success.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=6pt]
\item \textbf{Run it on a real router.} Cross-compile \texttt{aqosd}, \texttt{aqos-classify} and the watchdog for an OpenWrt router (ARM), which already ships CAKE and nftables. \emph{Test}: the same scenarios through the router at 25, 100 and 300\,Mbit/s, measuring CPU, memory, classification latency and throughput. \emph{Success}: under 10\% of one router core at 100\,Mbit/s, no loss of throughput against plain CAKE, and the same wins as in emulation.
\item \textbf{Test with real applications, live.} Real Zoom, Meet, WhatsApp and cloud-gaming sessions on laptops and phones over Wi-Fi, next to real backups and downloads, instead of generated or replayed traffic. \emph{Test}: read the apps' own quality statistics (WebRTC \texttt{getStats}: frame rate, freezes, bitrate), so the result includes the apps lowering their quality. \emph{Success}: fewer freezes and a higher sustained bitrate than plain CAKE, on the same evening's traffic.
\item \textbf{A field trial in homes.} Five to ten volunteer homes for four weeks, switching between AQoSE and plain CAKE on alternate days so each home is its own control. \emph{Measure}: router-side tin delay and probe delay, an opt-in note from users after calls (``did the call stutter?''), and the router's action log. \emph{Success}: fewer reported bad calls on AQoSE days, and no home worse off.
\item \textbf{Fix the two known gaps.} Send interactive flows above a set share of the line to Video instead of Voice, and calm the reclassifier for flows the model is unsure about. \emph{Test}: rerun S7 with 20 to 30\,Mbit/s cloud games and the v1 real-call runs; then repeat the whole frozen protocol.
\item \textbf{Broaden the data.} Captures from the team's and volunteers' homes across Indian ISPs and current apps, at least five sources for every class. \emph{Test}: leave-one-source-out damage below 0.5 for every source, and a new sealed test set opened once.
\item \textbf{Stress and coverage.} A flood of short flows, many devices at once, IPv6 on every scenario, and gigabit lines. \emph{Success}: never worse than plain CAKE in any of them.
\item \textbf{Make it usable.} A page in the router's web interface (LuCI) to prioritise a device for an hour, and a readable log of why each connection was placed where it was.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
Home routers already have a very good queue manager in CAKE, and AQoSE does not try to replace it. It answers the one question CAKE cannot: which of the connections on a busy device is the call or the game. A small, calibrated tree model that reads only the headers of a connection's first eight packets is enough to answer it, in about six microseconds, and the answer is written once per connection as a label the kernel already knows how to use. No AQoSE code sits on the per-packet path.

The evidence, all of it in emulation, is consistent. Where CAKE's fairness cannot help, a call or a game sharing its device with bulk traffic, late frames fell from 83--94\% to around 1\% across the main scenarios, on ten seeds and again on five fresh seeds the system had never seen. Real WhatsApp video calls, replayed packet for packet, were recognised within a fraction of a second and their late frames fell from 57\% to 2.4\% and from 79\% to 14\%. Where CAKE already works, AQoSE ties it, including on a home it was never tuned for. In 140 paired runs it was never worse than plain CAKE, never worse than the threshold rules existing tools use, and better than cake-autorate in all 20 runs where both were tested. On the sealed test set of real traffic it labelled 97\% of flows correctly and sent no call or cloud game to the slow lane, where qosify's defaults sent 78\% of cloud-gaming flows.

The work also showed what does not carry the result. The expected-damage rule is a sound way to combine uncertain predictions with measured costs, but in live tests the classifier did the work: recognising the traffic at all is what fixes CAKE's blind spot. The model is much weaker on data sources it has never seen, CAKE's tin shares limit what any label can do for very large flows, and every result so far comes from one laptop.

The system is built to fail safe: every change passes a fuzz-tested shield, is read back, can be undone by a verifier that sees only what a router sees, and a separate watchdog returns the router to plain CAKE within about two seconds. That makes the next step low-risk: a real router in real homes, where the question is no longer whether the idea works in emulation but how much it helps people on their own calls.
