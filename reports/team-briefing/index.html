The problem

Your video call freezes because your own laptop is busy

Picture this: you are on a video call, and the same laptop is uploading photos to Google Drive, or downloading a big game update. The call starts to freeze and stutter, even though your internet plan is fast enough for both.

Home routers decide which data goes out first. The best free software for this is called CAKE. CAKE is fair: it splits the internet line evenly between devices, and then evenly between each device's connections. That works well most of the time. But when your laptop has twenty connections open for the upload and one for the call, the call gets one twentieth of the laptop's share. That is not enough for video.

CAKE does have four priority lanes, called tins: Voice (fastest), Video, Best Effort (normal) and Bulk (slowest). The trouble is that something has to tell CAKE which connection is the call. Existing tools guess with simple rules such as "lots of packets per second means it's a download". Video calls and cloud games also send lots of packets, so those rules push exactly the wrong things into the slow lane.

Our idea

Recognise each connection, then pick its lane

AQoSE (Adaptive QoS Engine) is an add-on for routers that already run CAKE. CAKE keeps doing all the actual traffic handling. AQoSE only does one thing: it tells CAKE which lane each connection belongs in. It does this for every new connection, in four steps.

  1. Listen

    It looks at the first 8 packets of a new connection: how big each one is and how far apart they come. Only these outside details, never the content. Think of recognising a friend by their footsteps without seeing their face.

  2. Predict

    A small trained model (37 decision trees, made with a tool called LightGBM) turns those numbers into odds for seven kinds of traffic: voice call, game, video call, streaming, web browsing, download and background transfer.

  3. Decide

    It picks the lane that would do the least harm, given those odds. How bad each wrong choice is was measured in our tests, not guessed. Safety rules sit on top: nothing goes to the slow lane unless the model is over 80% sure, never on its first packets alone, and never a UDP connection, the kind calls and games use.

  4. Watch

    Every second it checks that each connection still behaves like what it was labelled. If it changes, it is checked again. If a change made delays worse, the change is undone.

An example of "least harm". Say the model is 55% sure a connection is a backup and 45% sure it is a game. Putting it in the slow lane is a gamble: if it really is the game, the game becomes unplayable. So AQoSE keeps it in a protected lane. If it is the backup after all, the backup only runs a little slower. A small cost, instead of a ruined game.

The safety net. Every decision passes a fixed safety check before it is applied, and a separate "watchdog" program puts the router back to plain CAKE within about 2 seconds if AQoSE crashes. So the worst case is what people have today.

An honest note from the reviews: most of the improvement comes from the model recognising the traffic at all (step 2). The "least harm" rule in step 3 adds a further gain and mainly acts as a safety refinement. In the write-up we lead with the recognising.

How we tested it

A whole fake home network inside one laptop

We could not test on hundreds of real homes, so we built a testbed: a pretend home network that runs inside one laptop. It has pretend devices (a laptop, a PC, a TV, a phone), a router, an internet line that we can make fast or slow, and pretend servers on the internet side. Programs on the devices act like real apps (video calls, games, downloads, streaming) and record whether every single packet and video frame arrived in time. People call this emulation.

We wrote 9 home situations (called scenarios, S1 to S10) and ran each one with 5 different router setups, 10 times each with different random details (called seeds). The same seed gives exactly the same traffic for every setup, so the comparison is fair: same test, different router.

Each run lasts 200 seconds in real time, and there were 450 of them, about 27 hours of testing. We also ran 90 A/A runs: the same setup twice with the same seed, to see how much results wobble by chance. Answer: almost not at all. In 8 of the 9 situations, two identical runs gave exactly the same score.

Why the ties are good news. In S1, S3, S4 and S10 each call or game is alone on its device. CAKE's fairness already protects it there, so nobody can do better than CAKE, and AQoSE doesn't get in the way. Our measurements predicted this before those tests ran. So the claim is: AQoSE beats CAKE when a call or game shares its device with heavy traffic, and ties it otherwise.

The model on its own. On 43,221 samples of recorded traffic it was never trained on, the current model labels 97% correctly. On apps from a data source it never saw at all, it does much worse, which is our main known weakness (see below).

Lessons

What went wrong along the way, and what it taught us

  • A wrong label is not always a wrong lane. Calling a video call a voice call does no harm: both are protected. What matters is where things end up, so we measure that.
  • Our harm table was wrong three times, and each time a test situation caught it. That is why the situations exist.
  • Tests we wrote ourselves shared our blind spots. The line-speed checker passed every simulated test and then failed four times on the real test network. All four are now permanent tests.
  • "Public data" does not mean "good data". One famous dataset sent all its streaming through a proxy and all its downloads over SSH, so a model trained on it learns the lab, not the apps.
  • When there's nothing to go on, do nothing. An early version slowed the line when its speed checks went quiet; that broke a call on a perfectly good line. Now it holds still.

Writing about it

What we can say, and what we must not

Safe to say

  • "Beats plain CAKE when a call or game shares its device with heavy traffic; ties it otherwise."
  • "Never did worse than the threshold rules in any test."
  • "Recognises traffic from packet sizes and timing; never reads content."
  • "Falls back to plain CAKE within about two seconds if it fails."
  • "In emulation" or "in a test network", every time we quote a result.

Do not say

  • "Beats CAKE", without the condition. Or "CAKE is bad": CAKE is our foundation.
  • "Deployed", "tested in real homes" or "ready for homes". It has not run on a real router yet.
  • "AI", "deep learning" or "neural network". It is decision trees.
  • "Seven (or eight) times better" as a headline.
  • That the WhatsApp calls were labelled correctly. The video calls were labelled as voice calls, though every one was still protected.

Weak spots

What the results do not show yet

  • Everything ran in emulation on one laptop. No real router, no real home, no Wi-Fi.
  • Our fake calls don't lower their quality when squeezed, the way real apps do, so CAKE's failures look bigger than they would be in real life.
  • The model is much weaker on apps from sources it never saw. The new safety rule keeps those out of the slow lane, but a call could still be left in the normal lane. The real-call replay tests on Friday check this directly.
  • The system was partly tuned on the same situations it is tested on. The fresh repeats and the brand-new situation (S11) in the final tests are there to answer this.
  • Some traffic types have little data: only 19 voice-call and 23 streaming connections in the check set.

Words you'll see

A small glossary

Packet
A small piece of data. Everything on the internet travels as packets.
Connection (flow)
One conversation between an app and a server, such as one video call or one download. Apps often open several.
QoS
Quality of Service: deciding which traffic goes first when the line is busy.
CAKE
The best free traffic manager for home routers, built into Linux. Fair to devices and connections, but it can't tell which connection is the call.
Tin (lane)
One of CAKE's four priority lanes: Voice, Video, Best Effort, Bulk.
Classifier (model)
The trained program that guesses what kind of traffic a connection is.
Late frames
Video or game frames that arrive too late to show. The main measure of a frozen or stuttering call.
Scenario
One home situation we test, such as "video call while the laptop shares files". S1 to S13.
Seed
A number that fixes all the random details of a test, so every router setup gets exactly the same traffic.
A/A runs
Running the exact same test twice to see how much results vary by chance.
Freeze
The point after which the system is not changed any more, so the final tests are fair.
Emulation, testbed
Our pretend home network inside one laptop. Real software, pretend devices and internet line.
Sealed test set
Real recordings kept aside and never looked at, opened once at the end for an honest score.
Damage, cost
Our score for how badly a connection suffered: 0 is fine, 1 is unusable.
